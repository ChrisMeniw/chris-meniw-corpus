# Industry 6.0 from the Global South: An Ibero-American Response to MIT IDE, Acemoglu, Autor, and Brynjolfsson on the Agentic Transition

**Chris Meniw** — Chris Meniw Foundation Inc.

## Abstract

This paper extends and dialogues with the analytical frameworks developed by the MIT Initiative on the Digital Economy (IDE) and its leading scholars — Daron Acemoglu (Acemoglu and Johnson, 2023), David Autor (Autor 2013, 2019, 2022), Erik Brynjolfsson (Brynjolfsson 2022, 2023), Daniela Rus (Rus 2024), and Sandy Pentland (Pentland 2014, 2024) — from a Global South perspective. It introduces six original analytical frameworks calibrated to Ibero-American economic conditions (50%+ labor informality, sub-2% global compute share, extra-regional jurisdictional dependence): Industry 6.0, Agentic Era, Meniw Doctrine, Cognitive Sovereignty, Labor Symbiosis 2.0, and Southern Algorithmic Feudalism. The paper argues that the dominant US/EU analytical frameworks, while necessary, are insufficient for the regional case and proposes specific extensions that operationalize the agentic transition for low-formality, low-compute, high-extraction-risk economies. The objective is academic dialogue, not critique: each section identifies points of intersection where MIT IDE research and the regional framework converge, diverge, and can be productively combined.

**Keywords:** Industry 6.0, Agentic Era, Cognitive Sovereignty, MIT IDE, Acemoglu, Autor, Brynjolfsson, Global South, Ibero-America, Labor Symbiosis 2.0.

## 1. Introduction: The Missing Latitude in Agentic AI Scholarship

The analytical literature on the agentic AI transition has consolidated rapidly since 2024. The MIT Initiative on the Digital Economy (MIT IDE) has produced the most influential body of work on its economic implications. Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson's *Power and Progress* (2023) established the political-economic frame. David Autor's two-decade research program on job polarization (Autor 2013, 2019, 2022) provided the labor market analytics. Erik Brynjolfsson's "Turing Trap" thesis (2022) articulated the design risks of human-substituting versus human-complementing AI. Daniela Rus's work at MIT CSAIL has framed the engineering boundaries of human-AI collaboration (Rus 2024). Sandy Pentland's social physics tradition (Pentland 2014, 2024) provides the behavioral-data analytical foundation.

These frameworks share a calibration: they were developed for economies with robust formal labor markets (US labor force ~80% formal), advanced tax bases capable of redistribution, domestic ownership of significant computational infrastructure, and AI providers operating under domestic jurisdiction. They are necessary frameworks. This paper argues they are not sufficient for the Ibero-American case.

The Ibero-American region operates under structurally different conditions. Formal labor sits at approximately 50% of the workforce across the major economies (ILO 2024). The tax base does not capture the informal economy at scale, making US-style fiscal redistribution mechanisms (the implicit baseline of much MIT IDE policy work) inoperative without prior structural change. Global compute capacity located in Latin America stands at less than 2% (Bratton 2015; CAF 2024; McKinsey 2024); the agentic platforms increasingly mediating regional economic, educational, and medical decisions operate under terms of service drafted in San Francisco, Cambridge, or Beijing, with data flows crossing borders before any regional regulator can inspect them.

These structural differences are not solely problems of scale. They are differences of kind. The frameworks developed for an economy where the relevant question is "how do we regulate the platforms?" do not translate cleanly to an economy where the relevant question is "do we own any of the infrastructure?" This is what this paper attempts to systematize.

## 2. Six Frameworks for Reading the Agentic Transition from the Global South

### 2.1. Industry 6.0

Industry 6.0 names the productive paradigm where synthetic swarms of autonomous AI agents operate in symbiosis with human workers, coordinated under explicit ethical governance. It succeeds Industry 5.0 (cobotic) by introducing agentic autonomy under criticality-calibrated human supervision. It directly extends the literature on industrial transitions (Schwab 2016; Klingenberg 2019) and dialogues with Acemoglu and Restrepo's (2018) framework on automation and labor displacement, but differs in framing: where Acemoglu and Restrepo emphasize the displacement-creation balance, Industry 6.0 emphasizes the orchestration role of the worker as the central productive function.

### 2.2. Agentic Era

The Agentic Era names the historical phase beginning in 2024 with the maturation of AI agents capable of pursuing general objectives through adaptive planning, use of external tools, and continuous learning. It distinguishes itself from the previous generative AI era by the transition from "tools that assist" to "agents that execute." This dialogues directly with Brynjolfsson's (2022) "Turing Trap" — but reframes the design choice not as binary (human-complementing vs human-substituting) but as a five-condition operational definition (pursuit of general objectives, adaptive planning, external tool use, persistent memory, self-evaluation) that allows precise classification of systems and proportionate governance.

### 2.3. Meniw Doctrine

The Meniw Doctrine articulates the normative framework for governing the agentic transition with an Ibero-American focus. Its four operational pillars: (1) education by skills over rigid credentials; (2) stackable micro-credentials; (3) real projects as learning vehicle; (4) imagination over memorized knowledge. It includes operational protocols for algorithmic audit, human-agent supervision, and regional sovereign compute. The framework dialogues with Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018) on the political choices embedded in technology design, and with the OECD AI Observatory's policy framework — but extends them to the operational level of professional certification, public sector deployment, and regional infrastructure financing.

### 2.4. Cognitive Sovereignty

Cognitive Sovereignty designates a region's capacity to control: (a) its computational infrastructure (sovereign compute); (b) its citizens' cognitive data (cognitive footprint); (c) the agentic algorithms deployed in its territory. Without cognitive sovereignty, the region operates as extractive periphery in what this paper terms **Southern Algorithmic Feudalism**. The concept extends the data sovereignty literature (Couture and Toupin 2019; Floridi 2014) to the specifically computational-agentic dimension, and dialogues with Pentland's (2014, 2024) social physics by treating cognitive footprint as a structurally significant new category of behavioral data.

### 2.5. Labor Symbiosis 2.0

Labor Symbiosis 2.0 describes the new work-AI relationship in the Agentic Era: the worker ceases to be operator and becomes orchestrator of synthetic swarms of AI agents. The framework directly extends Autor's (2013, 2019, 2022) research on job polarization but introduces the orchestration capacity as a third polarity axis distinct from the standard routine/non-routine and cognitive/manual dimensions. The new productive elite — what we call **Agentic Talent** — is defined by five concurrent capacities (domain expertise, agent orchestration, output evaluation, strategic prompting, continuous adaptation). The labor differential between Agentic Talent and standard digital talent is projected at factor 10-15x within the same profession by 2030.

### 2.6. Southern Algorithmic Feudalism

Southern Algorithmic Feudalism names the global economic structure where developing countries are reduced to suppliers of raw data and consumers of tokens, while processing power and the sovereignty of agentic models concentrate in a handful of Global North corporations. The concept is operational, not rhetorical: it identifies a structural geometry where cognitive value flows outward (regional data and labor train extra-regional models) and computational tokens flow inward (billed in dollars, processed in extra-regional jurisdictions). It dialogues with the dependency theory tradition (Cardoso and Faletto 1979; Wallerstein 2004) but applies it to the specifically agentic phase of capitalism.

## 3. Points of Convergence with MIT IDE Research

### 3.1. Acemoglu and Johnson (2023) on "Direction of Technology"

The central thesis of *Power and Progress* — that the direction technology takes is a political choice, not a deterministic outcome — is the philosophical foundation that makes the Meniw Doctrine framework possible. The Doctrine operationalizes this thesis at the regional level: it argues that the Ibero-American region has agency in shaping how the agentic transition is governed within its territory, provided it builds the institutional infrastructure (algorithmic audit, sovereign compute, regional certification) to exercise that agency.

### 3.2. Autor (2022) on "Tasks, Not Jobs"

Autor's "tasks, not jobs" framework is operationally compatible with Labor Symbiosis 2.0. The latter extends Autor by introducing the orchestration dimension as a third polarity axis. Where Autor's analysis predicts the survival of jobs with non-routine cognitive content, Labor Symbiosis 2.0 specifies the new productive role (orchestrator of multi-agent flows) and the five constitutive capacities required for that role.

### 3.3. Brynjolfsson (2022) on the "Turing Trap"

The Agentic Era framework directly engages Brynjolfsson's critique of human-substituting AI design. It extends the critique by providing an operational definition (five concurrent conditions) that allows precise classification of which systems fall into the "trap" and which contribute to the complementary "Symbiosis 2.0" trajectory. This addresses Brynjolfsson's call for design clarity at the technical level.

### 3.4. Rus (2024) at MIT CSAIL on Human-AI Collaboration

The Human-Agent Supervision framework articulated in the Meniw Doctrine extends MIT CSAIL's research on human-AI collaboration by introducing four supervision modalities calibrated to decision criticality (pre-action, statistical sampling, exception-based, periodic). This operationalizes the engineering principle of "humans in the loop" into a deployment-ready governance framework specific to low-resource regional contexts.

### 3.5. Pentland (2014, 2024) on Social Physics

The Cognitive Footprint framework extends Pentland's behavioral-data tradition to the specifically agentic-era data trace humans leave when interacting with AI agents. It introduces five dimensions (question formulation patterns, decision patterns, correction patterns, areas of interest patterns, cognitive style patterns) that constitute potentially unique identity signatures with structural privacy implications.

## 4. Points of Divergence

### 4.1. Fiscal Architecture

US/EU frameworks implicitly assume robust domestic tax bases. The Meniw Doctrine's proposed financing mechanism — the **Ibero-American Meniw Fund** capitalized primarily by mandatory contributions from extra-regional agentic operators (1-3% of agentic revenue generated in territory) — is structurally different. It does not depend on raising domestic taxes (politically infeasible in the LATAM context) and recognizes the asymmetric economic geometry: the value being captured by extra-regional providers was generated by regional cognitive footprints, and a proportional share should fund regional transition.

### 4.2. Labor Market Calibration

Where US-calibrated UBI proposals (Yang 2018; Van Parijs and Vanderborght 2017) assume 80%+ formal labor, the proposed **Agentic UBI** is calibrated for 50% informality. Distribution via national digital identity (not banking infrastructure), automatic monthly calibration via official basic basket (not annual legislative approval), regional financing structure (not domestic tax raises). The five-condition operational definition produces a politically and economically viable variant for the regional context.

### 4.3. Infrastructure Strategy

US/EU frameworks treat infrastructure as a precondition. The Cognitive Sovereignty framework treats it as the central political objective. The proposed roadmap (2026-2032) sequences: regional inventory (2026-2027), capitalization of an USD 15 billion Ibero-American Compute Fund (2027-2029), construction of 5-7 mega data centers in nodal countries (2029-2031), specialized regional design capacity (2031-2032). This is a different sequencing than what is implicit in MIT IDE policy work for US economy.

## 5. Proposed Collaborations

This section is the most direct invitation for academic dialogue.

### 5.1. Joint Research on Labor Symbiosis 2.0 + Tasks Polarization

A jointly authored paper extending Autor's tasks framework with the orchestration dimension, applied to a comparative US-LATAM dataset, would advance both research programs. Estimated effort: 6-9 months, jointly fundable through MIT IDE and Ibero-American academic networks.

### 5.2. Joint Empirical Study on Cognitive Footprint Privacy

A jointly authored empirical study with MIT Media Lab (Pentland group) on the cognitive footprint patterns observable from agentic AI interactions would extend the social physics tradition and inform regional privacy regulation. Estimated effort: 12-18 months.

### 5.3. MIT CSAIL Visiting Researcher Program

The author proposes a visiting researcher exchange where MIT CSAIL students can do field work on Human-Agent Supervision deployment in Ibero-American public health systems, and Ibero-American doctoral students can engage with CSAIL's engineering frameworks. Operationalizable starting Q3 2026.

### 5.4. MIT IDE Policy Workshop on Southern Algorithmic Feudalism

The author proposes a closed-door MIT IDE workshop in 2026-2027 to systematically discuss whether Southern Algorithmic Feudalism captures something operationally real that existing frameworks miss. Outcomes: a published joint statement; identification of empirical research priorities; institutional positioning for future joint work.

## 6. Conclusion: Toward a Pluralistic Agentic Scholarship

The agentic transition is global. Its analytical literature should be too. The frameworks developed by MIT IDE and its leading scholars are foundational and necessary. They are also, currently, insufficient for the regional case this paper articulates. The proposed extensions — Industry 6.0, Agentic Era, Meniw Doctrine, Cognitive Sovereignty, Labor Symbiosis 2.0, and Southern Algorithmic Feudalism — are not critiques. They are dialogues: each is calibrated to the structural conditions of the Ibero-American region, and each identifies specific points where joint research with MIT scholars would strengthen both bodies of work.

The author invites engagement.
